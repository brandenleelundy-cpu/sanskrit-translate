About this translator

A free tool for translating between English, Hindi and Sanskrit, with a romanized line under every Hindi and Sanskrit result so you can read it aloud even if you don't read Devanagari.

Start translating

Why Sanskrit

Sanskrit is the classical language of a vast body of literature, philosophy and science: the Vedas, the epics, the Bhagavad Gita, Pāṇini's grammar and much more. It is still studied, chanted and taught in schools, and a living community writes and speaks it today. Yet most translation tools treat it as an afterthought, if they support it at all. This project exists to make everyday English–Sanskrit translation easy to try, and to improve it over time.

How it works

Translations come from IndicTrans2, an open-source translation model released by AI4Bharat at IIT Madras that covers all 22 scheduled languages of India. When you press Translate, your text goes to a small server that runs the model and sends back:

  • the translation in Devanagari script, and
  • a romanized version in IAST (the International Alphabet of Sanskrit Transliteration), the system scholars use to write Sanskrit in Latin letters.

English↔Hindi and English↔Sanskrit are translated directly. Hindi↔Sanskrit goes through English in two steps, so it takes a little longer and can lose some nuance.

We're also fine-tuning the model on additional English–Sanskrit text, and switch to the improved version only when it scores better than the original on test sentences it has never seen.

Tips for better translations

  • Write short, complete sentences. One idea per sentence translates far more reliably than a long, winding paragraph.
  • Put each sentence on its own line if you're translating several. Each line is translated separately and kept in order.
  • Avoid slang and idioms. "It's raining cats and dogs" won't survive the trip; "It is raining very heavily" will.
  • Spell names the common way. Unusual spellings of people and places can come back garbled.
  • Check anything important. For a tattoo, an inscription, a ceremony or anything published, ask a Sanskrit teacher or scholar to review the result. Machine translation is a starting point, not a final authority.

Reading the romanized line

IAST uses a few marks that change the sound. Once you know them, you can pronounce most words correctly:

MarkSounds likeExample
ā, ī, ūlong vowels: "father", "machine", "rule"dhanyavād
ṛa vowel "ri", as in "Krishna" (kṛṣṇa)amṛta
ś, ṣboth roughly "sh"śānti
ṭ, ḍ, ṇ"t", "d", "n" with the tongue curled backsṭeśan
ṃ, ṁa nasal hum on the vowel before itkahāṁ
ḥa soft breath after the vowel, echoing itdhanyavādaḥ
calways "ch", as in "church"ca ("and")

For Hindi, the romanized line follows the same system, so it sometimes shows a final "a" that isn't pronounced in everyday speech (for example relave for रेलवे, said "relve").

Questions

Is it free?
Yes. The site may show a small number of ads to help cover running costs.
Why is the first translation sometimes slow?
The translation server sleeps when nobody has used it for a while. The first request wakes it up, which can take a minute or two; after that, translations take a few seconds.
Is my text stored?
Our code doesn't save what you translate. See the privacy policy for details, and please don't enter passwords or other sensitive information.
Can it translate verses and classical texts?
It can try, but it's best at everyday prose. Classical verse uses poetic word order, compounds and meter that a general-purpose model often flattens. Treat its output on scripture and poetry as a rough gloss.
I found a bad translation. Can I report it?
Please do: open an issue at our GitHub page with the text you entered, what came back, and (if you know it) what it should be. Examples like these are exactly what we use to improve the model.
